Week 1 · Day 1 · 01-01

Account safety and the IAM model

Everything here was done on your own account. The job of day 1 is to make the root user useless to an attacker, and to create a second identity for daily work that can be restricted.

Root is the problem

An AWS account begins with exactly one identity, created from the email you signed up with.

root

use 1–2 times a year

  • Can do anything
  • Can change billing details
  • Can close the account permanently
  • No policy can restrict it

yash-admin

use every day

  • Limited by policy
  • Can be restricted at any time
  • Cannot close the account
  • Can be deleted in 5 seconds if leaked

Both are logins to the same account. They are different identities, not different accounts. That separation is the whole point of IAM: who are you and what may you do are two different questions.

If root credentials leak you do not lose some data — you lose the account, including the ability to get back into it.

Why can root not be restricted?

Because something must be able to recover the account when everything else is misconfigured. If a policy could lock root out, one bad policy could make an account permanently unusable with no way back. Root is the escape hatch — and an escape hatch with a lock on it is not an escape hatch.

Access keys: a password for code

MFA guards a human at a sign-in page. Programs cannot type passwords, so AWS has a second kind of credential.

Access key ID:      AKIA...
Secret access key:  wJalr...

Anything holding that pair is that identity. No browser, no phone, no prompt. MFA does not protect it, because MFA only guards the sign-in page — and an access key never goes near one.

Root must never have access keys. Ever.

There is no valid reason for one to exist. Bots scan public commits for these strings and find them within seconds. This is the single most common way AWS accounts are compromised — not password guessing, a key pasted somewhere public.

checked on this account:
  root        0 access keys
  yash-admin  0 access keys

Confirmed by looking, not assumed. That distinction matters more in security than anywhere else.

Read an ARN

Every single thing in AWS has an Amazon Resource Name. Click any part.

Pick a piece Click any coloured part of the ARN above.

The empty slot where a region would go is meaningful, not a formatting quirk — IAM is global. Compare an S3 or EC2 ARN and a region appears. You will meet ARNs constantly: policies reference them, errors quote them, the CLI prints them.

Regions: where things live, and vanish

AWS is split into regions — separate physical locations, each with its own datacenters. Switch the console below.

EC2 · Instances —

The instance never stopped. You just stopped seeing it.

It is still running and still billing. This is the most common and most expensive beginner mistake in AWS — resources abandoned in a region nobody looks at, charging for months.

The same trap, a second shape

CloudTrail Event history is per-region too. Your CreateUser event did not appear in Mumbai, because IAM is global and logs to us-east-1.

you were viewing:  ap-south-1  (Mumbai)
IAM logs to:       us-east-1   (N. Virginia)

"I can't find it" almost always means "wrong region". Chosen for this account: ap-south-1 — nearest to you, and keeping everything in one region means nothing gets abandoned somewhere forgotten.

A budget is a smoke alarm, not a sprinkler

The most important thing to understand about AWS and money.

budget at $5  →  you spend $5  →  AWS emails you
                               →  AWS keeps charging
                               →  nothing is stopped

AWS will never cut off your services for cost. It cannot distinguish a student whose account was compromised from a company having its busiest day of the year. Stopping the second would be far more damaging than the bill, so AWS alerts and never enforces.

Billing data also lags up to 24 hours, so the alert describes yesterday.

So what actually protects you?

✗Budget alert — tells you, slowly
✓Tearing down every lab the same day — the real protection
✓Checking billing weekly — catches what you forgot
✓No access keys anywhere — closes the usual attack
✓Budget actions — the real circuit breaker · day 2

A zero-spend budget was chosen over a $5 monthly one: with $100 of credits a $5 threshold would never fire — a smoke alarm with the battery out. Zero-spend alerts on any charge above $0.01, the moment something starts costing rather than a week later.

Two switches that surprise everyone

Billing is a separate permission

A full administrator gets access denied on billing pages unless a separate switch is on. The reasoning is sound: a company may want twenty engineers with wide infrastructure permissions and none of them seeing invoices or card details. Admin over resources is not the same trust as seeing the bill.

On this account it was already active — newer accounts often ship that way. Confirmed by testing the behaviour rather than hunting for the setting, which is the faster move whenever a console menu has moved.

MFA needs a spare

AWS allows up to 8 MFA devices per identity, and any one of them signs you in. Two were registered on root: the authenticator app on the phone, and a passkey on the laptop.

phone dies  →  laptop passkey still works
laptop dies →  phone app still works

A passkey cannot be phished. A fake AWS page can ask for a 6-digit code and you might type it; a passkey is cryptographically bound to the real domain and simply refuses to work anywhere else.

This trade-off repeats everywhere in security and in AWS: strong protection makes lockout easier, and the answer is never weaker security — it is more than one way in, each one strong. You will meet the same shape again on day 14, deliberately killing an availability zone.

Words you now own

root
The original account identity. Unlimited, unrestrictable, barely used.
IAM user
A second login to the same account that can be limited.
group
A container of users sharing permissions. Change once, everyone updates.
policy
The document stating what is allowed or denied.
access key
A password for code. No MFA protects it.
MFA
A second factor at sign-in. Up to 8 devices, any one works.
passkey
Device-bound MFA that cannot be phished.
region
A physical location. Resources exist in exactly one, visible only from there.
ARN
The unique address of any AWS resource.
CloudTrail
A recording of who did what. Per-region; global services log to us-east-1.
budget
A cost alert. Never stops spending on its own.

Test yourself

Answer before looking anything up. A question you can only answer by re-reading is one you have not learned yet.

  1. Root cannot be restricted by any policy. Why would AWS design it that way, rather than letting you lock it down?
  2. You have MFA on root. Someone obtains a root access key. Are you protected? Explain precisely why or why not.
  3. Your budget is $5. You spend $40 overnight. What does AWS do?
  4. Given that, what actually protects you from a large bill?
  5. You are a full administrator but the billing page says access denied. What is going on, and why does AWS separate these?
  6. You launch an instance in Mumbai. Tomorrow the console shows none. Give two explanations and say how you would tell them apart.
  7. You created an IAM user 20 minutes ago, but CloudTrail in Mumbai does not show it. Why not?
  8. Read this and say what it is and where it lives — and why one field is empty:
    arn:aws:iam::639679388494:user/yash-admin
  9. You are the only person on this account. Why put yourself in a group rather than attaching the policy directly?
  10. Why a zero-spend budget here instead of a $5 monthly budget?
  11. AdministratorAccess is close to root power. Name two things it still cannot do — and why that matters.
  12. Your authenticator app entry is deleted by accident. Walk through getting back in, for yash-admin and then for root.
