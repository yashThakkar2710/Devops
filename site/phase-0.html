Journey of a request

Everything on this page comes from the capture you recorded in lab 00-01: 33 packets, 299 milliseconds, one visit to example.com. Nothing here is an example — it is your machine.

What is a network?

Start here. Nothing below assumes you know anything.

A network is two or more computers that can send each other messages. That is the whole definition. Everything else in this curriculum is detail about how that happens reliably, quickly and securely.

You need one because the thing you want — a web page, a file, a message — is sitting on a different computer than the one in front of you.

They are not plugged into each other

Your laptop is not wired to a server in another country. The message travels in short jumps, machine to machine:

your laptop172.26.1.224
→
home routerthe box on your wall
→
your ISPAirtel, Jio, …
→
≈ 10 moreundersea cables, other countries
→
the server104.20.23.154

No machine in that chain knows the whole route. Each one knows only where to pass the message next — like asking for directions at every corner instead of memorising the map. That single idea is what allows the internet to be enormous and to keep working when parts of it break.

What physically travels

Electricity down copper wire. Light down glass fibre. Radio through the air. They look completely different, but all three carry the same thing: ones and zeros. On or off, over and over, very fast.

Everything above that — every address, every page, every video call — is agreements about what particular patterns of ones and zeros mean.

Why it is cut into pieces

A message is not sent in one go. It is cut into small chunks, and each chunk is called a packet. Three reasons:

The line is shared

Everyone on the internet uses the same cables. If one person sent a 2 GB file as a single unbroken stream, everyone else would wait for it to finish.

Losing a little beats losing a lot

Something in the middle will occasionally drop a packet. Resending one small chunk is cheap. Resending a whole film is not.

Pieces can take different routes

If one path gets congested or breaks, later packets simply go another way. Nothing has to be restarted.

It is moving house. You cannot post a sofa, so you send many labelled boxes, and the person at the other end puts everything back together.

So what is "the internet"?

Many separate networks — your home, your office, a university, a hosting company — that have agreed to pass each other's packets along. There is no central computer running it. It is an agreement, kept by millions of machines independently.

Every line has two numbers on each side, separated by a dot. The first says which computer. The second says which program inside it.

Port 53 means name lookups everywhere in the world. Port 443 means encrypted web, everywhere. These are fixed agreements, not settings on your machine — which is the only reason a computer you have never met knows what you are asking for.

Your own port, 59573, was picked at random a moment before you sent the question. It exists so that when an answer arrives, your computer knows which of its running programs was waiting for it.

The name lookup, both halves

Two packets. A question and its answer. Notice what swaps between them.

0 ms
Out172.26.1.224.59573 > 1.1.1.1.53  A? example.com.

Where does example.com live?

83 ms
In1.1.1.1.53 > 172.26.1.224.59573  A 104.20.23.154

At 104.20.23.154.

The addresses and ports are identical in both lines, just reversed. That reversal is the whole mechanism: it is how your computer matches an answer to the question it asked, with no memory of the conversation required anywhere in between.

Your capture actually shows this happening twice — once because the lab asked 1.1.1.1 directly, and again because curl then made its own lookup through WSL. The second one asked two questions at once: A? for an old-style address and AAAA? for a new-style one. It received both, and used the old-style one. Why it does that is a Phase 2 question.

Where the 299 milliseconds went

All 33 packets, drawn to scale. Each block is real elapsed time from your capture.

259 ms of getting ready. 11 ms of actually talking.

Finding the address, agreeing to connect, and proving identity took 87% of the time. The page itself — the thing you wanted — arrived in the last few percent. Most of what makes the modern web fast is the long fight to shrink those first three blocks.

Words you now own

packet
One small chunk of a message. Big things are cut into many.
IP address
Which machine. 172.26.1.224 is yours; 104.20.23.154 is the server you reached.
port
Which program inside that machine. 53 is name lookups, 443 is encrypted web.
DNS
The system that turns a name into an address.
capture
A saved recording of packets. Yours is out/capture.pcap.
tcpdump
The tool that records packets and prints them as text.
interface
A network connection on your machine. eth0 goes outside; lo loops back to itself.

tcpdump -r out/capture.pcap -nn port 443only encrypted web

What the flags mean

-r
read from a saved file instead of the live network
-nn
show raw numbers — do not turn addresses into names or ports into service names. Without this, tcpdump makes extra lookups that pollute what you are trying to watch.
-i any
record from every interface at once (used by capture.sh)
-w file
write packets to a file instead of the screen
