Week 1 · Day 3 · 01-03

A VPC from scratch

AWS runs millions of servers in one building — yours, mine, a bank's. A VPC is a private network of your own inside it, sealed by default. Nothing reaches your servers unless you deliberately build a path.

Every book has a page 5

Nothing else makes sense until this clicks.

Millions of books, millions of "page 5", and no confusion — because page 5 means nothing until you say which book.

company A's network    company B's network    your network
10.0.0.5               10.0.0.5               10.0.0.5

All three exist. None collide. Now try to reach one from outside:

you     → "send this to 10.0.0.5"
router  → "which 10.0.0.5? there are millions."
router  → drops it

Private addresses are safe because the question is unanswerable.

Not because of a firewall. Before any rule exists, your servers have addresses the internet physically cannot deliver to.

Three ranges reserved worldwide

10.0.0.0    → 10.255.255.255
172.16.0.0  → 172.31.255.255
192.168.0.0 → 192.168.255.255

Anyone may use them. Nobody routes them. A public address is the opposite — unique on the whole internet, like a street address rather than a room number.

One machine, many addresses

Measured on this very PC.

Wi-Fi                       192.168.31.9     on the home network
vEthernet (WSL)             172.26.0.1
vEthernet (Default Switch)  172.20.144.1
Loopback                    127.0.0.1        itself

Four addresses, one computer. An address belongs to a door, not to a machine.

Three networks, nested — and the sender gets rewritten twice

leaving the inner network   FROM 172.26.1.224   TO 104.20.23.154
leaving the PC              FROM 192.168.31.9   TO 104.20.23.154   rewritten
leaving the router          FROM 152.59.39.80   TO 104.20.23.154   rewritten

The destination never changes. The sender changes at every boundary. The website only ever sees the public address, and could not reply to the private one if it tried.

Why rewrite? A private address is a page number — useless on the next network out. Send it unchanged and the reply has nowhere to go. Each layer keeps a note of who asked, so replies find their way home. That mechanism is NAT, and it is day 4.

The slash number

Drag it. Blue means fixed.

1st2nd3rd4th
/24

An IPv4 address is four numbers, each 0–255. The slash says how many of them are pinned down. Pin more, and fewer addresses are left to vary.

192 . 168 . 31 . 9  /24
└── fixed ───┘   └┬┘
                 varies

Bigger slash, smaller range. That feels backwards until you see it as "how much is fixed". And /0 fixes nothing at all — which is why 0.0.0.0/0 matches every address that exists, and is the catch-all in a route table.

Neighbourhood and streets

VPC     the neighbourhood   10.0.0.0/16    65,536 addresses
subnet  one street in it    10.0.1.0/24    256 addresses

Why cut it into streets? Not for tidiness — for different rules.

10.0.1.0/24    web servers   the internet may reach these
10.0.11.0/24   database      the internet must never reach these

Put everything on one street and you cannot separate them.

Availability zones are real buildings

A region is several availability zones — physically separate datacenters, different power, kilometres apart, joined by fibre fast enough that traffic between them takes about a millisecond.

A subnet lives in exactly one AZ and cannot span two. So surviving a datacenter failure needs at least two subnets, one per building.

Same shape as the MFA backup on day 1: more than one way in, each one strong. AWS repeats this idea everywhere.

The CIDR can never be changed

Chosen at creation, fixed forever. Blocks can be added; the first one cannot be altered or shrunk. Choose generously — unused addresses cost nothing, and a /24 VPC runs out.

Splitting a block

Knowing sizes is half of it. Carving a block into pieces that do not overlap is the half that matters. Change the numbers and watch.

The method is three steps

1. how many pieces do I want?
2. total / pieces = size of each
3. what slash is that size?   then count up by that size
256 -> /24   anchor. halve the size, add 1 to the slash.
128 -> /25
 64 -> /26
 32 -> /27
 16 -> /28

Do not compute powers of two — count steps from the anchor. That method survives exam pressure; recalling 2^n under time does not.

AWS takes five addresses from every subnet

x.x.x.0     network address
x.x.x.1     the VPC router
x.x.x.2     DNS
x.x.x.3     reserved
x.x.x.255   broadcast
usable = total - 5

A /24 gives 251 usable, not 256. Smallest subnet AWS permits is /28 (11 usable); largest is /16. Size by instinct rather than arithmetic and you discover the problem at server number 252.

The slip worth noticing now

10.0.5.0/25   starts at .0, extends 128  -> .0 to .127
10.0.5.0/24   starts at .0, extends 256  -> .0 to .255, the whole street

The address says where a subnet starts. The slash says how far it extends. Getting the starting addresses right and the slash wrong produces subnets that overlap — and on paper it looks completely fine.

The exam wants the minimum. Real life wants what you will not regret.

Six subnets of 500 servers needs /23 each and a /20 VPC — that is the exam answer. In practice you still choose /16: unused addresses cost nothing, a VPC can never be enlarged, and being wrong is cheap in one direction and unfixable in the other. Know both answers and why they differ.

What actually makes a subnet public

Toggle the two things below and watch which subnets can reach the internet.

ap-south-1a  ·  building A
public-1a 10.0.1.0/24—
private-1a 10.0.11.0/24—
ap-south-1b  ·  building B
public-1b 10.0.2.0/24—
private-1b 10.0.12.0/24—
no internet gateway

There is no "public" checkbox. The name means nothing.

A subnet is public if its route table has a line pointing to an internet gateway. It is a property of the route table, not of the subnet.

A route table is a signpost you have seen before

Destination     Target
10.0.0.0/16     local          my own neighbourhood → direct
0.0.0.0/0       igw-xxxxx      everything else → the gate
on any ordinary machine:
default  via 192.168.31.1      ← "default" IS 0.0.0.0/0

The table picks the most specific match. 10.0.1.50 matches /16 and stays local; 104.20.23.154 matches only 0.0.0.0/0 and goes to the gate.

local appears automatically and cannot be deleted. A VPC must always be able to talk to itself.

Never put an internet route in the main route table

Every VPC has a main route table — the fallback for any subnet not explicitly assigned one. Add an internet route there and every subnet becomes public, including ones you create next month and forget about.

The main table is what you get when you forget something.

So it should be the safe option. Public subnets get their own table; private subnets stay on the main one.

What exists now

my-vpc           10.0.0.0/16
  public-1a      10.0.1.0/24    ap-south-1a
  public-1b      10.0.2.0/24    ap-south-1b
  private-1a     10.0.11.0/24   ap-south-1a
  private-1b     10.0.12.0/24   ap-south-1b

my-igw           attached
public-rt        0.0.0.0/0 → my-igw   → public-1a, public-1b only
main route table only "local"         → both private subnets

Addressing scheme: low numbers public, teens private. Nothing technical about it — it means an address read in a log at 2am identifies itself without a lookup. Pick a scheme early and keep it.

No teardown today, and that is not a contradiction.

VPCs, subnets, route tables and gateways are free while idle, and this is the foundation for days 4 and 5. The teardown rule is about money, not tidiness: never leave running something that charges.

Words you now own

VPC
Your own private network inside AWS. Sealed by default.
subnet
One street inside it, living in exactly one availability zone.
CIDR
An address plus a slash saying how much of it is fixed.
private address
Reusable everywhere, routable nowhere.
availability zone
A physically separate datacenter within a region.
route table
The signpost telling a subnet where to send things.
local route
Automatic, permanent, "I know my own VPC".
main route table
The fallback for subnets you did not assign one. Keep it safe.
internet gateway
The gate out of the VPC. Useless until a route points at it.
0.0.0.0/0
Every address that exists. The catch-all, also called the default route.
NAT
Rewriting the sender address at a boundary. Day 4.

Test yourself

Answer before looking anything up.

  1. How can two companies both use 10.0.0.0/16 without any conflict?
  2. Your server is 10.0.1.50. Someone in another country types that address. What happens, and why?
  3. Why is a bigger slash number a smaller range?
  4. What does 0.0.0.0/0 mean, and why is it the catch-all?
  5. A packet is going to 10.0.2.99 and the route table has both 10.0.0.0/16 → local and 0.0.0.0/0 → igw. Which wins?
  6. You create an internet gateway and attach it. Can your servers reach the internet? Explain.
  7. What single change turns a private subnet into a public one?
  8. Why should an internet route never go in the main route table?
  9. Why can a subnet not span two availability zones?
  10. Why does a server in a public subnet still need something else before the internet can reach it?
  11. Your private subnet needs to download software updates. Why can you not just add a route to the internet gateway?
  12. Why can a VPC's CIDR never be changed after creation?
  13. A subnet is named public-1a and nothing can reach the internet from it. Give the most likely cause.
